## 1. Product summary

BioDex is a single-user Android app that turns a real-world life list into a Pokédex. The app ships with a curated catalogue for one region — the Pacific USA, roughly America west of the Rockies — organized into real ecosystems (coastal rainforest, high desert, mountain conifer forest, and so on), each with its own completion meter. The catalogue holds three kingdoms in one regional dex: 224 animals, 80 plants and 30 fungi, counted separately so that each list is its own completable game. Each species starts as a silhouette and "unlocks" when the user registers a photo of it from their phone's gallery. An unlocked entry shows the species' habitat, a canonical reference picture, an outbound link, and the user's own photos — which stay in the gallery and are linked, never copied. Plants additionally carry their **uses** — whether the species is edible or medicinal, which part, and when — which is also a first-class filter, so the dex doubles as a map of what is worth foraging-for-the-camera in each ecosystem. Identification is optional, opt-in per photo, and only for plants: pressing *Identify* sends one downscaled copy of that photo to Pl@ntNet and shows its candidates as a list to choose from. The app never picks one, and never says a photograph *is* a species. For animals and fungi there is no provider — the user identifies with Google Lens or their own knowledge and registers by name. Anything photographed that isn't in the catalogue becomes the user's own entry, auto-populated from public sources behind a single confirmation step. Unlike a wildlife-ID or foraging app, the point is the collection, the ecosystem meters, and the small thrill of an unlock. Built for one person, offline-first, and intentionally simple.

## 2. Core concepts / domain model

**Region** is the top-level scope of a catalogue, and a regional dex is named `<Region> BioDex`. v4 ships exactly one, the **Pacific USA BioDex** (region id `pacific`, display name "Pacific USA"). The region is visible in the UI as a pill beside the product name — the app is called BioDex, the region says which BioDex you are looking at — and it keys the catalogue so a second region (an "Eastern USA BioDex", say) can be added later as content, not schema.

**Kingdom** is the coarsest split inside a region: **animal**, **plant** or **fungus** (D27). All three live in the same regional dex, share the same ecosystems, the same grid, the same register flow and the same detail screen, but they are **counted separately** — the region's header carries one meter per kingdom ("47/120 animals · 3/80 plants · 0/30 fungi") and every ecosystem meter has a fraction per kingdom. The animal count the user has already started filling is untouched by the plants arriving beside it.

**Ecosystem** is a first-class sub-grouping within a region — a real ecological community, not just a filter tag. The Pacific USA region ships seven: Coastal Rainforest, Shoreline, Dunes & Kelp, Oak Woodland & Chaparral, Riparian & Wetland, High Desert & Sagebrush, Sierra & Cascade Mountains, Urban & Suburban. Two of these were deliberately renamed in v4 — "Rocky Shoreline & Kelp" and "Sierra/Cascade Alpine" in v3 — so that they describe where a plant grows as well as where an animal lives; their ids are unchanged (see D16). **A species can belong to more than one ecosystem** (a Coyote is at home in high desert, oak woodland, and the suburbs; a Ponderosa Pine spans the mountains and the high desert's edge), and a caught species counts toward every ecosystem it belongs to. Ecosystem totals therefore sum to more than the catalogue size; each ecosystem's meter is internally consistent ("Coastal Rainforest 12/24 · 2/15") and that is what matters.

**Class** is the grouping under a kingdom that the stats screen's bars and the Class filter use. For animals it is the taxonomic class the app has always used: bird, mammal, reptile, amphibian, fish, insect, other invertebrate. For plants it is **growth form, not taxonomy**: tree, shrub, herb, fern (see D13). Every class belongs to exactly one kingdom, so choosing a class implicitly chooses a kingdom — which is why there is no kingdom filter of its own (D28).

**Species** is one kind of living thing in the catalogue — "Western Screech-Owl", "Pacific Madrone". A curated species carries reference data (names, habitat text, image URL, outbound link, ecosystem tags, and for plants uses) that the app links to or streams but does not own. A **user-added species** is one the user registered that isn't in the base catalogue. The user supplies the two things only they have — the name and the photo — and the app auto-populates the rest from public sources when online: kingdom, accepted scientific name and class from GBIF, and habitat text and a canonical image from Wikipedia (see D10). Nothing is saved until the user accepts a confirmation card. Ecosystem tags, and for plants the growth form and the uses, are fields the user always picks by hand. User-added species get U-numbers after the catalogue, are visually marked, and count toward their kingdom's completion meter once caught — on both sides of the fraction, so the total grows with them (see D29, which reversed D9 on this point).

**Uses** are a plant's documented human uses — **edible**, **medicinal**, both, or neither — plus a short curated note naming which part is used and when ("Berries, late summer — cook first") and carrying any caution. The two tags have different provenance and the app never presents them identically. **Medicinal is sourced**: Dr. Duke's Phytochemical and Ethnobotanical Databases (USDA, CC0 — one downloadable file of 82,873 ethnobotanical use records over 13,010 taxa) is joined at build time on the GBIF-accepted name and its synonyms, and the entry shows the record count and the activities Duke's lists, attributed. **Edible is curated**: Duke's holds almost no food records, so the fruit-bearing and edible plants stay editorial judgment in the input list, as ecosystem tags are. Duke's `Poison` records are used at build time as a checklist — every species with one must carry a `Caution:` sentence — rather than shown as a tag (see D14). Uses were plant-only through v17; **v18 opens the edible tag to animals** (D48) — game, the shellfish and the insects that are eaten — while medicinal stays plant-only, because Duke's is a plant database. Both tags are filterable from the filter row like ecosystems and classes.

**Capture** is one registration event: the user attached one gallery photo to a species at a moment in time. The photo is referenced by a persistable content URI into the gallery; the app stores a small thumbnail of its own but no full-size copy (see D6).

**Entry** is the user's relationship to a species: it exists once the species has at least one capture, i.e. the species is "caught". One species has zero or one entry; one entry has one or more captures.

**Dex** is the whole collection: the region's catalogue plus the user's entries and user-added species, with progress derived per kingdom as caught-curated over total-curated, plus per-ecosystem and per-class breakdowns.

Relations in one line: Region 1—N Ecosystem; Region 1—N Species; Species N—M Ecosystem; Species 1—0..1 Entry; Entry 1—N Capture. Kingdom and class are attributes of Species, not tables.

## 3. Requirements

Each requirement is testable as written. M01–M21 and S01–S08 are shipped; v4 adds M22–M30, S09–S10 and C07–C08, and amends the shipped ones where plants change what they mean.

### MUST

- **M01** *(revised, see D32, D39, D41, D42, D44, D46, D47)* — The dex screen shows every curated species as a grid, ordered by name or by dex number at the user's choice — a choice that is remembered, settable from the grid or from Settings, and name order until they change it; every species as one picture filling its cell with the name written across the foot of it, caught ones in full colour (falling back to the user's own thumbnail when the picture has not cached, or leading with it where the entry prefers it — M46) and uncaught ones dimmed to grey, with the silhouette as the fallback for both — under a header naming the product, the region, and one completion meter per kingdom.
- **M02** *(revised, see D29)* — User-added species appear in the same grid after both curated lists, visually marked as user-added, and counted in their kingdom's progress numbers on both sides of the fraction: two of the user's own birds over a 120-animal catalogue read `2/122`.
- **M03** — Tapping any species opens its detail screen.
- **M04** *(revised, see D34)* — The detail screen for a caught curated species shows: the canonical picture, common and scientific name, its ecosystem tags, a world range map, habitat text, the user's own photos newest-first, and an outbound "Learn more" link. A plant additionally shows its uses section (M24).
- **M05** *(revised, see D34, D52)* — The detail screen for an uncaught species shows the reference picture greyed and faded — the silhouette where there is no picture to draw (D52) — with the name, ecosystems, the range map, habitat text, a plant's uses section, and offers a Register action. The map is not withheld the way the picture is: where a species lives is the information that makes it findable, and withholding it would work against the one thing the screen is for.
- **M06** — *Removed in v5.* Call playback: "tapping the call control plays the species' call audio". The whole feature is gone (see the header note).
- **M07** — The Register a Species screen searches the catalogue — all three kingdoms — by common or scientific name substring, entirely offline, then lets the user attach one photo via the system photo picker **or take one with the in-app camera (M40)** and register. **A plant registers without a photograph of its own (M41)**: the photo, when there is one, exists to identify the plant and is not kept. Animals and fungi keep theirs.
- **M08** — Registering a name not found in the catalogue offers "Add your own species", which starts the user-added flow (M18–M21) with that name and the attached photo.
- **M09** — Registering an uncaught species creates the entry and plays the unlock reveal (silhouette resolves into the photo, the kingdom's progress counter increments). Registering an already-caught species appends the photo with only a brief acknowledgment.
- **M10** — A registered photo is stored as a persistable content-URI reference into the gallery (persisted with `takePersistableUriPermission`); the app writes no full-size copy unless "keep a local copy" is on, and makes no network write of any photo or location **except a downscaled, metadata-free copy of a single photo when the user presses Identify on it (M36). Nothing is uploaded automatically, and neither the original bytes nor the EXIF location is ever sent.**
- **M11** *(revised, see D39)* — At registration the app generates and stores its own small thumbnail, so the detail screen renders without resolving gallery URIs and the grid has an offline fallback behind the reference picture. The grid never resolves a gallery URI.
- **M12** — If a photo's URI no longer resolves (deleted or moved in the gallery, or cloud-only and not downloaded), the entry stays caught: the app shows the stored thumbnail with a "full photo unavailable" state and offers to re-link a photo. It never shows a silent blank and never reverts the species.
- **M13** — Each capture records a timestamp from the photo's EXIF (falling back to registration time); location comes from EXIF only, and only if not declined.
- **M14** — Search finds species by name substring; filters narrow the grid by caught/uncaught, kingdom, use, class, and ecosystem. Search and filters compose (AND).
- **M15** *(revised, see D29)* — A stats screen shows overall progress per kingdom, a per-ecosystem breakdown with an animal meter and a plant meter per ecosystem, and a per-class breakdown grouped under Animals and Plants. A multi-ecosystem species counts in each of its ecosystems.
- **M16** — Browsing, search, filtering, the whole Register flow, and viewing entries (minus the remote image) work with no network connection.
- **M17** — Every third-party image displays or links to its attribution and license on the detail screen — auto-populated media on user-added species (the Wikipedia/Wikimedia image credit) exactly as much as curated media.
- **M18** — When online, adding a user species looks the name up: GBIF's species-match endpoint resolves it to a kingdom, an accepted scientific name, a class, and a match confidence, with alternative candidates when the name is ambiguous; Wikipedia supplies habitat text (the article's "Habitat" or "Distribution and habitat" section when one exists, else the summary lede) and a canonical image; for plants only, the bundled Duke's index is looked up **offline** by the accepted name and its GBIF synonyms for medicinal activities and any poison record. A species with no Duke's record simply lacks that line — a normal state, not an error.
- **M19** *(revised, see D43)* — Nothing is written until the user accepts a confirmation card showing the matched name (spelled as it will be stored — M45), scientific name, kingdom, class, found image and found habitat text; from the card the user can pick a different GBIF candidate or edit any field by hand. Ecosystem tags are never auto-derived: the card offers a manual multi-select (optional, defaulting to none). For a plant the card also offers the growth-form pick (defaulted from GBIF's class), the two use toggles and the uses note, none of which are auto-derived beyond the default (M27).
- **M20** — Offline, or when the lookup finds nothing, the species is created immediately from the name and photo alone and marked "details pending"; the app backfills automatically the next time it is online and the entry is opened, then presents the same confirmation card. Registration never blocks on the network.
- **M21** — Every auto-populated field remains editable afterward, and a field the user has edited is never overwritten by a later backfill.
- **M22** — Every species carries a kingdom. The grid header shows one completion meter per kingdom — animals `47/120`, plants `3/80` — and a species counts only toward its own kingdom's meter. The plant meter is hidden while the catalogue holds no plants; user-added species count toward neither.
- **M23** *(revised, see D28)* — The filter row is the caught chips (All, Caught, Uncaught) plus one dropdown per remaining dimension: **Class**, **Ecosystem** and **Uses** (Food source, Medicinal). There is no kingdom control. A dropdown's button carries the value currently filtering, its menu marks that value and opens with a row that clears just it, and the *All* chip clears every dimension at once. A use matches a plant tagged with that use (a plant tagged both matches either); animals and fungi never match a use. The Class menu offers every class the loaded region actually holds, so a catalogue with no fungi offers no Mushrooms. All four dimensions compose with each other and with search. The wire name behind the *Food source* label is unchanged (`edible`) in the asset, the database and the backup format (M42).
- **M24** *(revised, see D48)* — A **species** with something to say shows a section between the habitat text and the photo strip; an animal reaches it on D48's edible tag alone, and one with neither tag nor caution shows nothing. It shows, in this order: the use tags; any `Caution:` sentence emphasised in the stop colour **above** the rest of the note; the curated note; when the plant is medicinal, one line naming the source, the record count and the activities ("Duke's records 105 traditional uses: astringent, diuretic, wound…"); and the disclaimer of M30. The *Food source* tag and the note are visibly the app's curated content; the medicinal line is visibly a source's, in the attribution register.
  A species with **neither a use tag nor a caution** shows nothing in that slot: habitat is followed directly by the photo strip. A species with a **caution and no uses** — every fungus that carries one, and a plant such as Western Wild Ginger — shows the warning alone: the section is headed "Caution" rather than "Uses", and carries no disclaimer, because a line about documented uses says nothing where none are shown.
- **M25** — Uncaught plants render one of five plant silhouettes — conifer tree, broadleaf tree, shrub, herb, fern — chosen by class, with conifer/broadleaf decided per species by the pipeline from GBIF's taxonomic class. Animals keep their seven class silhouettes.
- **M26** *(revised, see D46)* — Plants are numbered `P001` onward in their own field-guide order (trees, then shrubs, herbs, ferns); animals keep `#001`–`#120`; the number marks the kingdom on detail headers, on the reveal and in search results. It is no longer drawn on a grid cell.
- **M27** — For a user-added species GBIF's kingdom decides which extra fields the confirmation card shows. A plant gets a growth-form picker (tree / shrub / herb / fern, defaulted from GBIF's class: conifers and other woody classes to tree, ferns to fern, else herb), an edible toggle (off by default), a medicinal toggle **defaulted from the bundled Duke's index** by the same three-activity rule the pipeline uses with the Duke's activities shown read-only beside it, and a uses note that is **pre-filled with "Caution: recorded as poisonous in Duke's ethnobotanical database."** when the index carries a `Poison` record for the species. The Duke's lookup is offline and needs no network. A details-pending species defaults to animal until backfill resolves its kingdom.
- **M28** — On the Register a Species screen the search field is pinned at the top, the photo row and the Register / Add-your-own actions are pinned at the bottom, and only the results list scrolls between them. With an empty query the list shows the whole catalogue in dex order, uncapped. Arriving with a pre-selected species, the list is scrolled so that species is visible and it is shown selected, with the Register button already naming it.
- **M29** — The app is named BioDex: launcher label, app bar title, share-sheet titles, backup archive identification and the About text all say BioDex, and the app bar shows the product name and the region name as two distinct elements — the title "BioDex" and a region pill "Pacific USA". Reading them together gives the regional dex's name, "Pacific USA BioDex".
- **M30** — The app's only claim about a plant's uses is that the use is *documented for the species* — by the curator (**food source**, the note) or by Duke's (medicinal), and the screen says which. A detail screen **that shows a use** carries a one-line disclaimer beneath the section ("Documented uses of the species — not advice. Do your own research before eating or using anything."), and the Settings About section repeats it. A caution-only section carries no disclaimer (M24). The app never states that a photographed individual is that species — an identification candidate is always shown as a named service's suggestion, and is never auto-selected — never states a part is safe, and carries no "safe to eat" wording anywhere.
  The longer statement is not in the app at all. It sits once at the top of the README: *this is intended as a fun activity, and nothing in it should be construed as medical or dietary advice.* Repeating a paragraph of it on three screens was ceremony, and it was cut on 2026-09-02 at the user's direction.
- **M31** — The Register screen offers, for an attached photo in a plant context, an *Identify* action naming the service it will call. It is hidden for animals and fungi, which have no provider. Pressing it uploads a reduced copy of that photo (M36) and shows the service's candidates as a list the user chooses from. The app never selects a candidate itself, and the typed-name path of M07 still works exactly as it does today for every kingdom.
- **M32** — Every candidate name is resolved through `GbifClient.match` before display, and only an `EXACT` or `FUZZY` match at species or subspecies rank survives — a `HIGHERRANK` match is treated as unresolved. A name that does not survive is not shown; the panel states how many were dropped. A candidate whose GBIF kingdom contradicts the context is dropped.
- **M33** — Catalogue matching is on the GBIF-accepted scientific name — genus and specific epithet, case-folded — never on common name. A matched candidate shows its dex number; an unmatched one offers "add as your own species", which opens the existing confirmation card (M19) with the GBIF lookup pre-filled.
- **M34** — The candidate panel is headed with the provider's name and states what kind of confidence it shows: a classifier's score as a percentage labelled as such; a language model's ranking, should one ever be added, as an ordered list with no number.
- **M35** — Fungi carry **no uses** — no *Food source* line and no medicinal line on any mushroom — and no identification. A fungal entry's only use-adjacent text is a curator's hand-written caution in `usesNote`, written only where the species itself is dangerous: ten of the thirty carry one, and the rest read like an animal. When there is one it renders (M24). Duke's is an ethnobotany database with no fungal taxa, so nothing sources these sentences and nothing can decide which species need them, which is why the app makes no claim about a mushroom beyond what the curator wrote by hand.
- **M36** — Upload is opt-in per photo (only on pressing Identify), and what is sent is a JPEG re-encoded from a decoded bitmap at reduced size, so it carries no EXIF and no location. The original file bytes are never sent. The licenses page, the About text and the README all say this.
- **M37** — Identification is counted per calendar month against a hard in-app cap (100, chosen as twice the expected volume and under every free tier; editable in Settings), the count is visible in Settings and on the disabled button, and the cap disables the action rather than warning past it.
- **M38** — Offline, without a key, or at the cap, the Identify action is disabled with the specific reason inline; nothing else on the screen changes. Identification outcomes are three-way: candidates, no candidates (ordinary, calm), could-not-ask (the only error state).
- **M39** — API keys are entered by the user in Settings and stored in app-private storage; no key is compiled into the build or committed to the repository. The field is masked with a reveal toggle, because a key that cannot be read back turns a typo into a mystery.
- **M40** — The Register screen offers an in-app camera via `ACTION_IMAGE_CAPTURE` writing to an app-cache `FileProvider` URI; the flow rejoins at `PickedPhoto`. Because the system camera app holds the permission, the app declares no `CAMERA` permission and shows no runtime prompt — verified on the phone. A photo for a kingdom that keeps photos is promoted into the gallery at registration; a plant's is deleted after registration or cancel and never enters the gallery.
- **M41** — A plant capture stores no photograph and no thumbnail; plant captures made before this shipped keep theirs. A photoless plant's grid tile and detail hero show the species' reference image with a path-neutral "caught — no photo of your own" mark, on accent chrome that distinguishes it from an uncaught tile whether or not the reference image loads. A plant registers with or without a photo on both the typed and the identified path, **and on the add-your-own path**, and whenever an attached photo would be discarded the screen says so before the user registers. A photoless capture is never offered a photo viewer or a re-link, is never counted as a missing photo in an export, and takes no URI grant.
  The rule is enforced in `AddSpeciesRegistrar` and in the Register screen rather than on one screen alone, so no door into the store can violate it. The capture row is still written — a caught species is caught — it simply has no photo.
- **M42** — The plant uses section labels the curated use **"Food source"**; the wire name `edible` is unchanged in the asset, the database and the backup format.
- **M43** — Every species carries its Linnaean path — kingdom, phylum, class, order, family — from the same GBIF match the pipeline already makes. A screen reachable from the grid's top bar and from a species' own entry names the three species in the catalogue closest to it and how many hops through that path separate them: 2 the same family, 4 the same order, 6 the same class, 8 the same phylum, 10 the same kingdom, 12 nothing but life. A rank GBIF leaves blank is recorded blank and never guessed at. A species with no path at all — every user-added one until its backfill — shows no neighbours and says why.
- **M44** — A caught species whose grid cell is showing a silhouette rather than a picture carries a filled green tick, where one showing a picture carries the quiet outline tick. The grid does not distinguish *why* there is no picture — a capture that never had a photograph of its own, one whose gallery photo was deleted or had its permission withdrawn, one whose reference image has not cached — because at thumbnail size the only honest statement is "you have this one, and the picture is not here". The entry screen explains which it is, and offers the re-link where that is the answer.
- **M45** — A species the user adds has its names spelled the catalogue's way before they are shown or stored: every word of the common name capitalised and continued in lowercase, lowercase after a hyphen, the short joining words (of, the, and, …) lowercase unless they lead; the scientific name with the genus alone capitalised. The confirmation card shows the formatted spelling, and an open hand-edit field shows what is being typed. Inner capitals are given up on purpose ("McKay's" becomes "Mckay's") — the rule is small enough to predict.
- **M46** — A caught species carries a choice of which picture leads its grid tile and its hero: the catalogue's reference picture (the default) or the user's own photograph. The choice is made on the entry screen, offered only when the species has both a reference picture and a thumbnail of its own, and the picture not chosen stays as the fallback. It is kept per species, survives a catalogue update, and travels in the backup archive.

## 4. The "catching" mechanic

Uncaught species render as flat dark silhouettes on the grid and in detail — present, named, but withheld. This makes the dex legible as a to-do list: you can see what is out there and what you are missing, which is the engine of the whole product. Ecosystem meters sharpen it: "High Desert 2/19 · 1/12" is an itinerary, not just a statistic — and with plants in the dex, the itinerary now works in every season: the trees and shrubs are there when the birds are not.

Registering is species-first: you already know what you photographed (Lens told you, or you did), you search the name, attach the photo, register. On first registration the silhouette holds for a beat, swells, and resolves into the user's actual photo as two rings push outward; the naming lines rise in one at a time; and the counter for that kingdom holds its old value and then ticks, with a second haptic on the number itself. About two and a half seconds, skippable at any point. Everything is drawn in the app's own accent on its own background — no confetti, no sound, no mascot (D8 as revised by D33). The user's own photo becomes the entry's face.

Repeat registrations of a caught species are deliberately low-key: the photo slides into the entry's strip with a brief "+1" toast. Ceremony is reserved for firsts, so firsts stay special. User-added species get the same reveal, with their U-number in place of a dex number.

Plants change the rhythm of the game more than its rules. A tree is caught on a walk with no luck at all; the plant list is the reliable half of the dex and the animal list the lucky half. Two separate meters are what let both feel like progress rather than the plants diluting an animal count the user has already started filling.

The tone target is "field naturalist's logbook with good game feel": tactile, satisfying, quiet. No XP, no streaks, no mascot.

## 5. Key product decisions

**D1** *(revised, see D50)* **— Seed the dex with curated regional lists (120 animals, ~80 plants), not a full taxonomy.**
Why: a dex must feel completable to work as a game. A full taxonomy makes progress read as 0% forever and the unlock loop dies. ~120 animals the user can realistically encounter west of the Rockies — common backyard and regional-park animals plus a tail of aspirational ones, invertebrates included — keeps every silhouette a plausible goal. The plant list follows the same rule at ~80: roughly 40 trees (the region has few enough that "most trees" is a real, finishable list), and among the rest the fruit-bearing, edible and medicinal species the user actually wants to find. The region is a first-class concept so more regions can ship later (C03).
Rejected: full taxonomy (unwinnable); dynamic list from an API (network dependency, unstable dex numbers, no offline guarantee); a plant list of every wildflower (hundreds of near-identical entries nobody would finish).

**D2 — Identification is a suggestion service the user invokes; the app never identifies.** *(v6; superseded "No in-app identification, deliberately.")*
Why: the typed-name path is unchanged (M07) and remains the whole story for animals and fungi. What v6 adds is a camera and an *Identify* button that send **this** photo to a named third party and show its candidates as a list to choose from — ranked, with the classifier's score shown as such (D22), validated through GBIF (M32) and matched to the catalogue on scientific name (M33). The app never auto-selects (D20) and never renders "this is *X*". The claim it makes is "Pl@ntNet suggested these", in the same attribution register as "Duke's records these uses": a source's statement, never the app's. There is no fungus or animal provider, so the button exists only for plants.
The original argument still holds where it applied — the app must not be the thing that told the user what a plant was — and is satisfied by the app never issuing a verdict rather than by refusing to ask.
Rejected: auto-selecting the top match (D10's argument, made stronger by a photo being less reliable than a typed name); an on-device classifier (heavyweight, and still no species-level model); a compare-against-reference confirmation gate on plants (the first draft of the identification design had one — cut as ceremony the user declined).

**D3 — Habitat text, descriptions, uses, the Duke's index and silhouettes ship with the app; canonical images are streamed then cached.**
Why: text is tiny and must work offline day one, so it is bundled — and uses text especially, since it is read standing in front of the plant. Duke's ethnobotanical file is CC0 and a couple of megabytes compacted, so the whole index ships in the APK and a user-added plant gets its medicinal activities and poison check with no network at all. Media is large and third-party, so it is loaded from source URLs and cached on first view (S02) — the field-use case degrades gracefully (placeholder + text) rather than failing.
Rejected: bundling all media (app bloat, and redistributing licensed media crosses from linking into copying); streaming everything always (dead in the field).

**D4 — *Removed in v5.*** Call audio streaming with cache-on-first-play. Gone with the rest of the call feature (see the header note); the app now streams no audio of any kind, and the Media3 player and its audio cache were deleted.

**D5 — Third-party media is linked/streamed with visible attribution; nothing third-party is redistributed.**
Why: sources like Wikimedia Commons are CC-licensed with attribution requirements, and some (e.g. Macaulay Library) do not permit redistribution at all. Streaming from source with an attribution line on the detail screen (M17) satisfies the licenses. Cached copies are private device caches, not redistribution.
Rejected: baking media into the APK (license risk, size); no attribution (violates CC-BY terms).

**D6 — Photos are referenced from the gallery by persistable content URI; the app stores no copy by default.**
Why: this is what the user asked for ("a link to Google Photos would be better than storing the photo"), and Android's own photo picker delivers it with no Google API at all: `PickVisualMedia` returns a content URI, and `takePersistableUriPermission()` with the read flag makes the grant survive reboots. The Google Photos API cannot do this — the `photoslibrary.readonly` scope was removed in April 2025, and its replacement Picker API returns only a `baseUrl` that expires after 60 minutes, with no durable link back to the library item — and it would have required OAuth and a Cloud project besides.
Tradeoffs, stated honestly: if the user deletes or moves the photo in the gallery the reference breaks — the entry stays caught with a "photo unavailable" placeholder and a re-link offer (M12), never a silent blank. A photo that lives only in Google Photos cloud storage and isn't on the device may not resolve offline. Android caps persistable grants at 5,000 per app, far above a personal life list but real. The app keeps its own thumbnails (M11) so the collection always renders, and the "keep a local copy" setting (S03, default off) is the durability escape hatch — default off because linking, not storing, is the point.
Rejected: Google Photos API links (technically impossible since April 2025, see above); copying every photo into app storage (duplicates the user's library and contradicts the ask); referencing without persisting the grant (breaks on every reboot).

**D7 — An entry's identity is the species, unlocked once; repeats accumulate under it.**
Why: this is the Pokédex frame — the list is of species, not of sightings. Sightings-as-primary is a journal (eBird and iNaturalist already exist); species-as-primary is a collection. Captures remain first-class underneath, so no data is lost.
Rejected: sighting-first journal model (different product); per-location or per-season re-unlocks (badges C02 can cover this itch later).

**D8 — The unlock reveal is restrained by design.** *(v9; the duration and "a card flip and a soft glow" are superseded by D33. v14: the no-fanfare rule is overruled by the owner — see D40. Sound and mascot commentary stay rejected.)*
Why: the user is an adult using this for years; a quiet, tactile reveal stays satisfying on the 90th unlock where fanfare would curdle.
Rejected: full-screen celebration animations *(reversed by D40)*, sound effects, mascot commentary.

**D9 — Ecosystems are a first-class dimension; species can belong to several; user-added species sit outside the meters.** *(v8; the user-added clauses are superseded by D29 — the ecosystem rules stand.)*
Why: ecosystems make the collection a map of places to go, and per-ecosystem meters give the dex seven small winnable games inside the big one. Multi-membership reflects reality (a Coyote genuinely spans three ecosystems) and costs nothing beyond the honest caveat that ecosystem totals sum past the catalogue — each meter is consistent within itself, which is what the user reads. User-added species are an addendum ("+3 of your own"), not part of either kingdom's count: letting them count would let the meter inflate itself and make "complete" meaningless.
A user-added species with ecosystem tags shows on that ecosystem's meter as an addendum ("12/24 +1"), leaving the curated fraction intact — same treatment as the overall meter's "+3 of your own". With two kingdoms, every ecosystem meter is two fractions, one per kingdom, each with its own addendum.
Rejected: single-ecosystem assignment (forces false choices for wide-ranging species); counting user-added species toward completion (destroys the meter); ecosystem as a mere filter tag (loses the per-ecosystem progress that makes it fun).
**The addendum clauses of this decision are superseded by D29** — the ecosystem rules are not. Multi-membership, the seven small games and the per-kingdom split all still stand; only "user-added species sit outside the meters" is reversed.

**D10 — User-added species auto-populate from public APIs, behind one confirmation step.**
Why: the user supplies the two things only they have — the name and the photo — and free public APIs supply the rest. GBIF's species-match endpoint is the spine: it decides which species the name means, returning the kingdom, the accepted scientific name, the class, and a confidence score, with alternatives when the name is ambiguous. Wikipedia supplies the prose and picture, preferring the article's "Habitat" or "Distribution and habitat" section over the generic lede, so an auto-added entry reads like the hand-written curated ones. The confirmation card exists because common names are ambiguous ("sparrow" matches dozens of species; "cedar" is two families) and a silent wrong pick corrupts the entry permanently: nothing is saved until the user accepts, swaps candidates, or edits. Ecosystem tags are a field no API can supply — nothing maps species onto these seven Pacific ecosystems — so they stay a manual multi-select on the card; for plants, growth form and uses are manual for the same reason (D13, D14).
Identity and travel: user-added species take U-numbers (U01, U02, …) in the order added and sit outside both meters. A holiday catch from outside the region lands here as an ordinary user-added entry with no out-of-region flag — the addendum is already outside the meter, so a flag would add complexity without changing anything the user sees. If one turns out to be a genuine missing Pacific native, a later catalogue update can promote it (C06).
Rejected: silent auto-accept of the top match (wrong often enough on ambiguous names to be destructive); fully manual entry (v2's design — undersold what the free APIs deliver); blocking registration on the lookup (the offline-first rule governs here too, hence "details pending" + backfill, M20).

**D11 — The product is BioDex; a regional dex is `<Region> BioDex`; the UI shows the two names as two things.**
Why: the app is no longer about animals, and the user wants the Pokémon shape — one product, regional dexes inside it. The app bar had a title ("Animal Dex") and a region pill ("PACIFIC") that read as one label; now the title is the product name, "BioDex", and the pill is the region's display name, "Pacific USA", so the pair reads "Pacific USA BioDex" without the title having to change per region. The region's display name comes from the catalogue (a `regions` table seeded from the asset), not from code, so an Eastern USA BioDex is a content drop. The app bar also carries one progress pill per kingdom, so it is now five elements wide; the rules for narrow screens are that pills drop their inner spaces (`47/120`), the plant pill is hidden while the catalogue has no plants, and if it still does not fit the title ellipsises before any pill does — the counts are the information, the title is the brand.
Rejected: "Pacific USA BioDex" as one long title (does not survive a second region and leaves no room for the meters); dropping the region pill because there is only one region (the point is to make the region legible as a thing that could be different); naming the animal half "Animal Dex" inside BioDex (two brands for one app).

**D12 — One regional dex, two kingdoms, counted separately; plants get their own number sequence.**
Why: the user has started filling a 120-animal meter, and folding 80 plants into it would turn "47/120" into "47/200" overnight — destroying a number that already means something. Two meters also match how the two halves are played (§4): the plant list is finishable in a season of walks, the animal list is a years-long luck game, and a single blended percentage would hide both facts. The kingdoms nonetheless share everything else — grid, ecosystems, register flow, detail screen, stats screen — because a Douglas-fir and a Douglas Squirrel are found on the same walk in the same ecosystem, and two separate apps-inside-an-app would double every screen. Plants are numbered `P001`–`P080` in their own field-guide order (trees, shrubs, herbs, ferns) so the number itself marks the kingdom on a grid cell; animals keep `#001`–`#120` exactly as they are.
Rejected: one blended 200-species meter (see above); separate tabs or a kingdom switcher (splits an ecosystem walk into two screens, and doubles the stats); numbering plants `#121`–`#200` (implies one list and would renumber if the animal list ever grows).

**D13 — Plant classes are growth forms — tree, shrub, herb, fern — and kingdom is stored on every species.**
Why: the stats screen's class bars and the filter chips must group plants in a way the user thinks in, and the user thinks "trees" — nobody filters for Magnoliopsida. Growth form is also what a silhouette can depict: a conifer, a broadleaf tree, a shrub, a forb and a fern are five recognisable shapes, where "dicot" is not a shape. Because growth form is not taxonomy, GBIF cannot supply it: like ecosystem tags it is curatorial input in the plant list, and a manual pick (with a sensible default) on the confirmation card. The one thing GBIF *can* decide is conifer versus broadleaf within trees, so the pipeline picks that silhouette automatically from GBIF's class (Pinopsida → conifer). Kingdom is stored as its own field rather than derived from class, because it is what the header meters, the kingdom chips and the pipeline's validation key on, and because a details-pending species has a kingdom before it has a class. Every class belongs to exactly one kingdom and the app enforces that pairing.
Rejected: taxonomic plant classes (meaningless to the user, and GBIF's plant classification is unstable — it has no consistent class for many flowering plants); three growth forms with ferns folded into herbs (a fern is the most recognisable plant silhouette there is, and the pipeline can tell ferns apart from GBIF's class Polypodiopsida for free); deriving kingdom from class at runtime (leaves a pending species classless *and* kingdomless).

**D14 — Uses are two differently-sourced tags and one curated note, with Duke's poison records driving which species must carry a caution — and the app is explicit about which claim is whose.** *(v18: the edible tag is no longer plant-only — see D48.)*
Why: food source and medicinal are the two things the user actually cares about in a plant, and they must be a filter, not a footnote, or the "which of these can I forage for the camera today" question the user is asking goes unanswered. The two halves have different provenance and the design refuses to blur them.
*Medicinal is sourced.* Dr. Duke's Phytochemical and Ethnobotanical Databases (USDA Agricultural Research Service) is public domain (CC0), a single 5.8 MB download rather than a per-species API, and its `ETHNOBOT` table holds 82,873 use records over 13,010 taxa keyed by genus and species with an activity name per record. Coverage against this region's plants was measured, not assumed: yarrow 105 records, nettle 79, elderberry 60, Douglas-fir 11, western redcedar 7, salmonberry 5, Oregon grape 4, devil's club 0, evergreen huckleberry 0 — roughly a fifth of the plants have nothing, which is a normal state. So the medicinal tag is *derived*: a species is tagged medicinal when Duke's records three or more distinct activities for it (a threshold, because Duke's is broad enough that a single stray record would tag nearly every plant), the curator can pin the tag either way, and the entry shows the record count and the activities so a 105-record yarrow and a 4-record Oregon grape read differently. It is presented the way the app already presents Wikipedia habitat text: fetched, attributed, bundled, shown as what a source records, never as the app's own claim. This replaces the v4 draft's hand-written medicinal notes, which would in practice have been written by an implementing agent from model knowledge — fluent, plausible, unverifiable, about something the user may put in their mouth.
*Food source is curated.* The tag was labelled "Edible" until 2026-09-02; the user renamed it because the app was over-weighting the edibility question, and the wire name `edible` is unchanged everywhere below the label (M42). Duke's is overwhelmingly medicinal — 15 `Food` and 14 `Fruit` records in the entire file — and no other public source is both structured and trustworthy. So the fruit-bearing and edible plants stay editorial judgment in the curated list, exactly as ecosystem tags are, with a short curated note naming the part and the season, because "edible" alone is useless in front of a plant whose leaves are eaten and whose berries are not. The pipeline pulls Wikipedia's "Uses" and "Culinary" sections into a curator review file to help write those notes, and never into the asset.
*Poison records drive the cautions, as a checklist, not a badge.* This is the one part of the caution machinery that survived the 2026-09-02 trim intact, because it is decided by a source rather than by a curator's mood. The same Duke's file carries 1,654 `Poison` records. A "toxic" tag on some species would imply the untagged ones are safe — the wrong inference for a foraging app — so there is no tag. Instead the pipeline treats a `Poison` record as a build-time requirement: every such species must carry a `Caution:` sentence in its note or the build fails, so the set of cautioned species is decided by a source rather than by whoever wrote the notes, and a plant with both a traditional use and a poison record — precisely the case a hand-written list misses — cannot ship without one. The curator still adds cautions Duke's cannot express (a lethal lookalike, a preparation hazard, a documented use the modern literature says not to follow). On a user-added plant, where no curator exists, the bundled index pre-fills the same caution sentence (M27).
The safety dimension, stated plainly: an app that says "food source" carries real-world risk. BioDex therefore claims only that a use is *documented for the species*, says by whom, does not identify the plant in the photo — it shows a named service's suggestions and never picks one (D2) — does not say a part is safe, and shows a one-line disclaimer under any section that displays a use (M30). Uses are plant-only: whether an animal is edible is a hunting and fishing regulation question, not a field-guide fact, and the app stays out of it. Fungi carry no uses either, for a different reason (M35).

*How much of this text there should be, decided 2026-09-02.* The first cut of the fungi catalogue gave every one of the 30 species a mandatory `Caution:` paragraph, and the plant notes averaged 28 words of look-alike keys and preparation hazards. The user's judgment, given twice: this is a collecting game, entries are a sentence or two, and the safety apparatus was overdone. So a note is now written **only when the species itself is dangerous**, in one short sentence, and it says what the thing does to a person rather than what it is mistaken for — a look-alike key belongs in a field guide and reads as noise here. Twenty of the thirty fungi carry nothing at all and read like an animal. The Duke's rule below is untouched by this, because it is source-driven and now costs four words; the rule that *required* a caution on every fungus is gone, and it is what had put a warning on the turkey tail and forced a sentence to be invented where no source said anything. The long-form statement moved to the top of the README (M30).
Rejected: hand-written medicinal notes (the v4 draft — see above); fetching edible from Wikipedia prose (unreliable, and puts free text behind a safety claim); a curated or sourced "toxic" tag or badge (see above); tagging medicinal on any single Duke's record (nearly every plant would qualify); per-species queries against a live ethnobotany service (there is none, and a bulk CC0 file is the better shape for an offline app anyway); Duke's phytochemical tables (chemistry, not use — out of scope).

**D15 — A plant's uses sit between the habitat text and the photo strip, and a plant with no recorded use shows nothing there.**
Why: uses are the kingdom-specific fact about a plant, and they belong with the rest of what the species *is*, above the user's own photos. A plant with no recorded use renders nothing rather than "No uses recorded" — an empty section on a Douglas-fir tells the reader nothing, and the plant's habitat text and photos are the whole of its story. (In v4 this decision was framed as the uses section standing where an animal's call row was; v5 removed calls, so the section simply follows habitat for every plant and animals have nothing there.)
Rejected: a "No uses recorded" line (see above); putting uses in the habitat paragraph (loses the tags, the caution styling and the filter's visual anchor).

**D16 — Two ecosystems are renamed so they describe where a plant grows, not only where an animal lives.**
Why: the seven ecosystems were named from the animal list. Checked against the plant list, five hold as written — Coastal Rainforest, Oak Woodland & Chaparral, Riparian & Wetland, High Desert & Sagebrush and Urban & Suburban all describe plant communities as well as animal habitats. Two do not. "Sierra/Cascade Alpine" names only the zone above treeline, and the montane conifer forest below it — Ponderosa Pine, Sugar Pine, Lodgepole, Red Fir, Giant Sequoia, Whitebark Pine, most of the forty trees — had no ecosystem at all; it becomes **Sierra & Cascade Mountains**, montane forest through alpine. "Rocky Shoreline & Kelp" describes the animal habitat by its cover and leaves out the dunes and bluffs where the shore plants (Beach Strawberry, Salal on the bluffs) actually grow; it becomes **Shoreline, Dunes & Kelp**. Kelp stays in the name because the kelp forest is still where the sea otters and rockfish are tagged, even though kelps themselves are not plants (C07). Ecosystem ids (`alpine`, `rocky-shore-kelp`) do not change — they are never shown — so the animal list's 120 tag sets are untouched.
Rejected: adding an eighth "Montane Forest" ecosystem (splits a single mountain walk into two meters, and the animal list's alpine species would need re-tagging); leaving the names alone and tagging the conifers "alpine" (wrong in a way a user who has stood in a ponderosa forest would notice).

**D17 — The rename installs fresh: no Room migration, no data carried over.**
Why: the package changes from `dev.tlong.animaldex` to `dev.tlong.biodex`, which to Android is a different app with its own sandbox, and the only data in the old one is a single throwaway test capture. So the v4 schema is designed as if starting from an empty database — new columns, a new table and a moved user-added number base — and ships as schema version 1 of the new app. The old app is uninstalled by hand. The implementers must **not** build migration machinery for this change: no `Migration` objects, no `fallbackToDestructiveMigration`, no import of the old database. The rule that every later schema change ships a real migration resumes the moment the first real capture lands in the new app.
Rejected: keeping the package and migrating (a migration for one throwaway row, and the applicationId would still say animaldex forever); an in-app "import from Animal Dex" (there is nothing to import).

**D18 — The Register screen pins search and actions and scrolls only the list.**
Why: the shipped screen is one scroll view — title, search, results, photo row, buttons — so with the empty-query behaviour listing the catalogue, the Register button sits 120 rows down, and arriving from a detail screen's "Register this species" puts the pre-selected species 40 rows down and out of sight: it looks like nothing happened. The fix is the layout every search-and-commit screen uses: search pinned at the top, the photo row and the two action buttons docked at the bottom, a lazy list scrolling between them, scrolled on arrival so the pre-selected species is on screen and marked selected. With the actions docked, the list no longer needs the 25-row cap that existed only to keep the buttons reachable, so the whole 200-species catalogue lists under an empty query. This bug would have bitten twice as hard with plants — the list is now 200 rows.
Rejected: keeping one scroll view and scrolling it to the button (the user still cannot see search and the selected species at once); collapsing the results when a species is pre-selected (hides the way to change the pick).

**D19 — One provider, Pl@ntNet, for plants only; no kingdom chip.**
Why: fungi and animals have no provider, so there is no routing choice to put in front of the user. A registry keyed by `Kingdom` keeps the shape for a later provider without asking for anything now. Google Lens was the original request and turned out to be impossible as framed — it has no public API, and its share intent is one-way — so the choice was a real classifier or nothing. Pl@ntNet is free for non-commercial use, returns scored candidates with scientific names, and in a 2023 comparison identified plants correctly far more often than Lens.
Rejected: a kingdom chip on the Register screen (a control with one working setting); shipping fungal identification (no provider we trust, and see D23).

**D20 — Candidates are chosen, never auto-selected.**
Why: D10's argument, strengthened by a photo being less reliable than a typed name.
Rejected: preselecting the top match with a "change" affordance (the preselection *is* the app's statement); auto-registering above a score threshold.

**D21 — One interface, `LookupResult` outcomes, a query producer.**
Why: identification yields names, and everything downstream already keys off a name.
Rejected: a new outcome type; an identification screen of its own; caching results across sessions.

**D22 — Confidence is rendered by kind.**
Why: a classifier score is a probability the user can weigh; a language model's "confidence" is a token it emitted. Every provider today is a classifier, so every score is a percentage; the self-report rendering stays specified for a future provider.
Rejected: a uniform "high / medium / low"; hiding scores altogether.

**D23 — No identification for fungi, and none for animals yet.**
Why: for fungi, a wrong mushroom name is the one wrong answer in this app with a serious consequence, and nothing here sources fungal claims (M35). For animals, no provider has been chosen. Neither is a permanent decision; both are the absence of a provider rather than a refusal to build the mechanism.
Rejected: a confirmation gate that would let fungal identification ship (an earlier draft had one, cut with the feature); the same gate on plants (cut at the user's direction as over-weighting plant edibility).

**D24 — Keys live in Settings, not the build.**
Why: the repository is public and an APK is unpackable, so a compiled-in key is a published key. The cost is that the feature ships dark until the user pastes one (R16).
Rejected: a build-time key from a git-ignored properties file (still ends up in the APK); a proxy service (a backend, which this app does not have).

**D25 — A plant capture keeps no photograph, and its tile is the species' reference image.**
Why: the user's own framing — for a plant the photo exists to identify it, not to be kept, and a catalogue reference picture is a better tile than a snapshot of a shrub. The hazard is that a caught plant rendered with the reference image alone looks like a *rich uncaught tile*, so the colour carries the state rather than the picture: accent chrome and a leaf glyph, applied whether or not the image actually loaded.
Rejected: keeping the photo (the user asked for the opposite); an animated generic leaf in place of a picture (loses the species); a "no photo" placeholder (reads as broken).

**D26 — The camera writes to app cache and promotes at registration.**
Why: a shot must not enter the gallery before the user commits, and for a plant it must never enter at all — but the kingdom is not known until a species is chosen. So the file waits in cache, and the screen that knows the kingdom decides: promote, or drop and sweep. On the add-your-own path that screen is the confirmation card, not the Register screen.
Rejected: promoting immediately on capture (puts a plant's photo in the gallery, and litters it on cancel); writing straight to the gallery via `MediaStore` (same problem, plus a permission).

**D27 — Fungi are a third kingdom in the catalogue, not a flavour of plant.**
Why: they are a different GBIF kingdom, a different provider, a different risk class and a different silhouette set. Folding them into `PLANT` would put mushrooms behind the Plant chip, in the plant fraction and under the plant provider, which does not identify them.
Rejected: a `taxClass = mushroom` under `PLANT` (every one of those consequences); shipping fungal identification against an empty catalogue (every candidate reads "not in dex", which is the feature not working).

**D28 — The filter dimensions are dropdowns, and kingdom is not one of them.**
Why: the chip row put five dimensions in one scrolling line, so with three kingdoms, two uses, fourteen classes and seven ecosystems in it, everything past "Uncaught" was off-screen and the row gave no hint of what it held. A dropdown per dimension names the dimension on the button, shows the value chosen, and fits the four of them on one line. Kingdom goes entirely: every class belongs to exactly one kingdom (§2), so *Mammals* and *Trees* already say it, and a separate kingdom control was a second tap for a narrowing the class dropdown does on its own. The three header meters still report per kingdom, which is where the kingdom split earns its place — as progress, not as a filter.
Rejected: keeping kingdom as a fourth dropdown (redundant with class, and the user asked for it gone); one dropdown holding all dimensions grouped under headings (a menu that long is the scrolling chip row again, one layer deeper); leaving the clear affordance implicit in re-picking the active value (invisible — the menus carry a real *All classes* row, see M23).

**D29 — A species the user added counts in the meters, on both sides of the fraction.**
Why: the owner registered two species of their own and the header still read `0/120`, which is the app telling them their morning's work did not happen. D9 kept them out to stop the meter inflating itself, but the inflation it feared never arrives — adding a species costs a photograph and a confirmation card, and the same person owns both the catalogue and the count. What was actually lost was the only feedback the register flow gives. So a caught user-added species is added to its kingdom's numerator *and* denominator, and to every ecosystem and class it belongs to: 120 curated animals plus two of the user's own is `2/122`. `Meter.userAdded` survives as a breakdown of the numerator, which the Stats summary line still reports as "2 of your own"; the trailing `+N` column beside each meter is gone, because it would count the same species twice on one row. An uncaught user-added species — only reachable through a backup import — enters neither side. "Complete" now means the catalogue plus everything you found that was not in it, which is a truer description of a life list than the catalogue alone.
Rejected: counting them in the numerator only (`2/120`, which lets the fraction pass 100%); a separate third meter for the user's own species (a third bar over two species, and the header has no room); leaving the addendum beside the new fraction (`2/122 +2` reads as four).

**D30 — Reference photographs are fitted into their frame, not cropped to fill it.**
Why: the hero on the detail screen and the mini-hero on the confirmation card are fixed-height letterbox frames, and the images the pipeline pulls from Wikimedia are whatever shape the photographer used. Filling the frame with a portrait bird photograph cropped the bird out of it, which is the one thing the frame exists to show. Fitting letterboxes the image against the frame's own background instead. The hero stops drawing its silhouette once the photograph is up, since a silhouette showing through the letterbox bands reads as a fault. The user's *own* thumbnails still crop: they are square tiles at 74dp and 92dp, the shot is the user's own, and a fitted thumbnail in a grid of cropped ones looks broken.
Rejected: a taller or aspect-ratio-driven hero (the frame's height is what keeps the detail screen's first fold readable); cropping with a smarter focal point (nothing in the pipeline knows where the animal is in the frame).

**D31 — The grid's Register button lives in the top bar, not in a floating action button.**
Why: the owner asked for it at the top. The FAB floated over the bottom-right tiles for the whole time it was not being used, which on a three-column grid is a species permanently half-covered, and it forced 88dp of empty padding under the last row so the grid could scroll clear of it. In the top bar it sits beside the settings gear, where the screen's other two controls already are, and the grid gets its bottom row back. It keeps the accent circle so it still reads as the one control that adds to the collection rather than as a third gear.
Rejected: a Register row pinned above the grid (a whole line of vertical space for one button); putting it in the bottom navigation bar (that bar names *places*, and Register is an action).

**D32 — The grid can be sorted by name, and sort is not a filter.** *(v16: name order is now the default — see D42.)*
Why: dex-number order is the game's order — it is what makes the grid a map of what is missing, and it stays the default. But 230 species is past the size where finding a particular one by eye is pleasant, and the dex is also a thing you look *up*. Name order is the second way to read the same list. It is a separate control from the filters because it does a different job: filters narrow, sort reorders, and the *All* chip that clears every filter deliberately leaves the order alone — someone reading alphabetically has not asked to be put back into catalogue order because they cleared a class. Name order is case-insensitive and breaks ties on the dex number: the user types the name on a species they add, and "oak titmouse" must file under O rather than after every capitalised name in the dex.
Rejected: sorting inside the filter row's *All* reset (see above); a sort that persists across launches (the dex should open in dex order — that is the game, and the alphabetical view is a lookup you enter deliberately); sort by date caught or by ecosystem (both are real, neither was asked for, and each new order is another thing the grid has to explain).

**D33 — The unlock reveal is a sequence, not a card.** *(v14: the accent-only palette and the rejection of confetti below are overruled by D40; the sequence, the timing and the fixed scatter stand.)*
Why: the reveal was doing nothing at all. `animateFloatAsState(targetValue = 1f)` builds its animation already at the target on first composition, so no frame of the intended cross-fade or settle ever ran, and what shipped was a still card holding for a second and a half — which is what "can we have more animation when I register a species" was actually reporting. Fixing it needed a real timeline, and given a real timeline the moment was worth building properly: the silhouette holds a beat and swells, the photograph resolves out of it while two rings push outward through a scatter of motes, the naming lines rise in one at a time, and the counter holds the old number before ticking to the new one with its own haptic. About two and a half seconds, skippable throughout. D8's reasoning is untouched and is why this is drawn only in the accent on the app's own background, with nothing that spins, bounces, makes noise or scatters differently each time: it has to be worth watching on the ninetieth unlock, and the thing being celebrated is a number changing by one.
Rejected: leaving the still card (it reads as a loading screen); confetti, colour outside the palette, or sound (D8, unchanged); randomised particles (a screen seen a hundred times should be the same screen each time); a longer hold (past about three seconds a reveal you cannot skip fast enough becomes something to tap through).

**D35** *(revised, see D37)* **— The launcher icon carries a thin white edge around the mark.**
Why: the mark is dark walnut and it was disappearing into a dark home screen. The edge separates it from the wallpaper without changing the mark.
Rejected: a white background with no inset (the art is opaque out to the canvas edge, so nothing would show); a monochrome/themed layer (a photographic mark cannot theme cleanly, so themed launchers fall back to the full-colour icon rather than showing a muddy silhouette).

The first attempt drew the edge by insetting the foreground 21dp over the white background layer, so that the background showed around it and took the shape of whatever mask the launcher applied. D37 records why that was replaced.

**D34 — Every species carries a world range map: one shared outline, per-species shading.**
Why: "where does this live" is the question a dex entry raises and the habitat paragraph answers slowly. A picture answers it at a glance, and it makes the region meaningful — a species found across three continents and one found in six Californian grid cells read as different animals the moment you see them side by side.

The map is a 128x64 equirectangular grid. The region carries **one** land outline for all of it, as a base64 bitmask of about a kilobyte; a species carries only the list of cells it shades. That is the whole design: the outline never differs, so it is stored once, and a species costs a handful of small integers rather than an image. It also means the land and the shading sit on exactly the same cell edges, which a traced coastline and a separate overlay would not.

**The shading is where the species has been recorded, not a range polygon from a field guide.** It comes from GBIF's occurrence density, so it shows where people have looked as much as where the animal is: somewhere nobody surveys is blank whether or not the species lives there. The caption under the map says so. This is a deliberate choice of an honest, already-attributed source over a prettier one, and nobody should later "fix" it into published range polygons without knowing they are changing both the meaning and the licence.

A single stray record — a museum specimen, a misidentification — is dropped by a threshold: unbinned point rendering makes one record a few pixels in a cell and a real population a saturated one, so a cell is kept when it is a fifth as covered as the strongest cell, or half full outright. The outline comes from Natural Earth, which is public domain. The obvious shortcut, deriving land from GBIF's own density with no taxon filter, was tried and does not work: strict thresholds hole out the Sahara and the Amazon where few people record anything, and loose ones fill the oceans with ship tracks.

A species with no records gets no map at all rather than an empty frame, which would say "this species lives nowhere". That covers every user-added species until its backfill.
Rejected: a vector world map (a real coastline asset, and the shading would no longer align with it); one raster per species (230 images in the APK to say what a list of integers says); published range polygons (a different licence, and not free); rendering the map only for caught species (M05 — where a thing lives is what makes it findable, and withholding that fights the point of the screen).

**D38 — A caught species with no picture is marked, not left to the chrome alone.**
Why: the owner deleted two photographs from the gallery months after registering them, and the app's answer was a cell that looked, at a glance, like a species they had never caught. The accent border and ground were doing the work of saying "caught", and they are too quiet to survive being scanned past at thumbnail size — especially beside a neighbouring cell that *is* a photograph. A filled tick is loud enough to read without being read.

**One mark for three causes, deliberately.** A plant that keeps no photograph of its own (M41), a gallery photo that has been deleted or had its permission withdrawn, and a reference image that has not cached while offline are three different situations, and the grid can tell them apart only in ways it should not act on. What they share is what the cell can honestly say — the species is caught and there is no picture here — and the entry screen is where the difference gets explained, because that is where something can be done about it.

**The rule takes the load result as a parameter; the chrome still refuses to.** `tileWearsAccentChrome` is deliberately blind to whether an image loaded, so that a failed fetch can never demote a caught species to looking uncaught (§5.3.1). The tick is the exact opposite: it exists to mark the load having produced nothing, so it must know. Both live in `TileState.kt` so that whoever changes one sees the other.
Rejected: a distinct mark per cause (three marks the user has to learn, to distinguish situations that call for the same next step — open the entry); a written label in the caption band (the band already carries the number and the name, and at 11sp a third line is noise); doing nothing on the grid and explaining it only on the entry screen (the entry is not where the species is scanned past).

**D39 — The grid draws the species' reference picture, not the user's photograph.** *(v15: the uncaught tile now draws it too, dimmed — see D41.)*
Why: the owner asked for it. Scrolling the grid, what they want to see is the animal, the plant or the fungus — the catalogue's Wikimedia picture, which is composed, lit and unmistakably that species — rather than their own shot of it, which at 74dp is often a brown smudge in a bush. The photograph is still the trophy, and it keeps the entry screen, the photo viewer and the reveal; the grid is a field guide of what you have, and the stock picture reads better there. The cell tries the reference picture first and falls back to the user's own thumbnail when the picture has not cached, so an offline grid still shows a picture where it can; only when both are gone does it draw the silhouette, with M44's filled tick. An uncaught tile was left on the silhouette here, and D41 reversed that the same day. The order is a pure list the JVM suite pins, and the chrome rule of §5.3.1 is unchanged — it still refuses to know whether anything loaded. The picture is cropped to fill the tile rather than fitted as D30 fits the hero, because a letterboxed photograph at 74dp is a stripe. `imageUrl` was already on every summary for M41's plant tile, so this costs the grid no new query. **The grid does not fetch `imageUrl` itself**: the pipeline recorded originals of up to 9 MB and 3840px thumbnails, and the first launch after D41 sat on silhouettes because 230 of those were queued at once. The cell rewrites a Commons URL to that file's 960px rendition — the smallest width Wikimedia will serve for these files — which is ~100–200 KB, and a cell whose every picture failed retries once after a few seconds, because Wikimedia renders a rendition on its first request and a burst of first requests times some out. The hero still shows the full picture.
Rejected: showing the reference picture on uncaught tiles too *(reversed by D41)*; a per-tile toggle or a setting (two grids to maintain for one owner's one preference) *(the per-species toggle is now D44)*; keeping the user's thumbnail first with the reference as fallback (that is the previous behaviour with extra steps — the ask was the stock picture, so it leads).

**D40 — The unlock reveal is a celebration.**
Why: the owner asked for colour and "something like a funfetti effect — it should be fun, not just another screen", which is a direct reversal of D8's no-fanfare rule and the part of D33 that kept the reveal to the accent on the app's own background. Both stand as records of what was believed; this records the owner overruling them. What was added: a burst of confetti in seven colours outside the palette, thrown up from the halo as the photograph lands and falling under gravity across the naming lines; a warm radial wash behind the halo that rises with the photograph; a rainbow ring turning once around the picture over the reveal; and the "NEW SPECIES" label in the theme's amber (the confetti gold itself is unreadable on the light background). What D33 keeps: the sequence and its beats, the rings and motes, the counter ticking last in the accent (the number is still the point), the fixed scatter so it is the same screen every time, tap-to-skip throughout, and no sound or mascot. The whole thing is two hundred milliseconds longer, to let the confetti fall.
Rejected: randomised confetti (D33's reason holds — a screen seen a hundred times should be the same screen); sound (not asked for, and a phone in a field is often on silent); adding the confetti colours to the theme tokens (they are the reveal's, and nothing else should reach for them).

**D41 — An uncaught tile draws the reference picture too, dimmed.**
Why: asked whether the stock picture should also stand on uncaught tiles, the owner said yes — with the picture dimmed. So the grid now works the way a locked entry does in any collecting game: every species shows its picture, an uncaught one drained to grey and faded to under half weight on the grey field, a caught one the same picture in full colour with the tick. The unlock is no longer a silhouette becoming a picture; it is a grey picture becoming a coloured one, which reads just as clearly at thumbnail size and tells the user what they are looking for. The silhouette stays as the fallback when no picture is available, dimmed the same way. *(Revised, see D52: the detail screen was left withholding the full-size picture here, and that split turned out to be the wrong answer — the entry now dims the same picture the tile does.)* The dimming is a function of the tile state alone, never of the load, and a caught tile is never dimmed for any reason.
Rejected: a blur instead of a desaturation (a blurred thumbnail at 74dp is a smear, and a greyscale one is still recognisably the species); a lock glyph on top (the grey already says it, and the cell has no room); keeping the silhouette for uncaught (the owner chose otherwise).

**D42 — The grid opens in name order.**
Why: the owner asked for the grid sorted alphabetically. D32 kept dex-number order as the default on the reasoning that the catalogue's order is the game's map of what is missing — but with every tile now carrying its picture (D39, D41) the grid reads as a map whichever way it is ordered, and what the owner actually does with it is look things up. Name order is case-insensitive and breaks ties on the dex number, as D32 says. Dex number stays one tap away in the Sort dropdown, and the dropdown's cleared state now reads as name order, since that is the order you return to.
Rejected: persisting the last-picked sort across launches (still not asked for; the default is now the order the owner wants, so the case for remembering the other one is weaker than it was); putting Dex number first in the dropdown (the list reads top-down, and the default belongs on top).

**D43 — A typed name is formatted at the door, and the card shows the result.**
Why: the owner asked that names of species they add be formatted automatically so naming is consistent, and the catalogue already has one convention — every word capitalised, lowercase after a hyphen, the small joining words lowercase — so the rule is that convention and nothing else. It lives in `SpeciesFields.normalized()`, which every write path already calls, so a name reaches the store spelled one way whether it came from the confirm card, an offline add or a hand-edit on a later backfill; and `previewFields` calls the same function, so M19's card previews the spelling that will be saved. The one place the formatted value must not appear is an open text field: a field bound to it swallows the trailing space the typist just entered and flips "o" to "O" and back on "of", so the hand-edit fields show the raw keystrokes and everything else on the card shows the result. Inner capitals are lost deliberately — a list of exceptions is a formatter nobody can predict.
Rejected: a formatter that preserves capitals already typed (then "BROWN PELICAN" stays shouted); asking on the card whether to apply it (one more thing to tap on a card that is already the whole flow); reformatting the species already in the store (the ask was for new species — raised as a question instead) *(reversed by D45)*.

**D44 — A caught species can lead with the user's own photograph.**
Why: the owner asked for a toggle on the entry screen to choose between their photo and the stock picture for that species. D39 rejected "a per-tile toggle or a setting" as two grids to maintain — but this is neither: it is one boolean on the entry, read by the same ordered picture list the tile already walks (`tileImageSources`), which simply puts the thumbnail first when the entry prefers it and keeps the reference picture as the fallback. The hero follows the tile, because "the picture for this species" means the same thing on both screens; at 158dp the 640px thumbnail is more than enough. The control appears only when there are two pictures to choose between — a photoless plant has nothing to switch to, and a user-added species before its backfill has nothing to switch from. The preference is a column on `entries` rather than a capture flag because it is a choice about the species, and which of the user's photographs it shows is still the favourite (S04). It rides in the backup archive beside the favourite, and a merge keeps the local choice.
Rejected: a global "always my photos" setting (the ask was per species, and the reason it was asked is that some of the owner's photos are better than the stock one and most are not); a flag on the capture (it would move when the favourite changed); hiding the toggle behind the photo viewer (it is about the species, so it sits under the species' picture).

**D45 — The species already in the store are respelled on the next start.**
Why: asked whether the names added before M45 should be reformatted too, the owner said yes. The sweep is the same formatter over the user's rows, run once per start after the catalogue import; it writes only the rows whose spelling would change, so it is idempotent and needs no "already done" flag — a flag is one more thing to migrate, and a sweep over a dozen rows costs nothing. It touches the two name columns and nothing else: `userEditedFields` stays as it was, so a hand-edited name stays locked against a later backfill, just respelled. Curated rows are never read — their spelling is the catalogue's.
Rejected: a Room migration (a data fix in a schema migration cannot be re-run, and the formatter may yet change); waiting for each species' next details card (the owner asked for it now).

**D46 — A grid cell is one picture with the name written on it.**
Why: the owner reported that a tile's photograph was cut off at the top and that the dex number was no use to them, and asked what the name overlaid on the picture would look like. Three things followed from one change. The caption band under the picture was taking 30dp out of a 104dp cell, so giving the whole cell to the photograph shows around 40% more of it. The crop moves to the top of the frame rather than the middle, which is what had been taking the heads off portrait-shaped photographs — a wildlife photograph is composed with its subject high far more often than low. And the name goes on the picture, at the foot, over a scrim that fades up from the bottom edge so it reads over a bright sky and a dark trunk alike.
The dex number goes with the band. It cost the second of three lines in a cell 11sp high and the owner does not navigate by it; it is still on the entry header, on the reveal and in the search results, which is where a number is actually read. That does cost M26's "the number is the kingdom mark on a grid cell" — a fungus and a plant no longer announce themselves on the tile — but the Class dropdown and the picture itself both say it, and a mark nobody reads is not a mark.
A cell that ends up drawing a silhouette keeps the name on the tile's own surface instead of a scrim: there is no photograph under it, and a dark band across a pale silhouette ground reads as damage rather than design. That is the one conditional, and it is a pure function ([tileLabelOnPicture]) so it is pinned by a test rather than by eye.
Rejected: keeping the number as a corner chip on the picture (the same information in a place that now costs picture); a taller cell (three columns of 230 species is already a long scroll); fitting the photograph instead of cropping it (D30 fits the hero for a reason that does not hold at 104dp — a letterboxed tile is a stripe); centre-cropping with a taller cell (the reported bug is about where the crop is taken from, not how much is shown).

**D47 — The grid's order is a saved setting, with two doors onto it.**
Why: the owner asked for a setting to choose what the grid sorts by, having found the order resetting to name on every launch. So the order becomes a preference in `AppSettings` rather than a field the ViewModel initialises — and the Sort dropdown on the grid writes that same preference instead of a value of its own. Two controls, one stored answer: picking Dex number from the grid is picking it in Settings, which is what "remember what I chose" has to mean if the two are not to disagree. The Settings card is a pair of chips rather than a dropdown, because a two-option list that a dropdown hides behind a tap is not worth the tap.
The preference is stored as the sort's wire name, and an unrecognised one opens in name order — a grid that will not open is worse than a grid in the wrong order. The grid collects the setting as a flow rather than reading it once, so a change made in Settings reaches a grid already composed behind it. And the dropdown's label now always names the order it is in: the old convention (blank unless it differs from the fixed default) stops meaning anything once the default is whatever the user last picked.
Rejected: a separate "default order" setting with the dropdown as a per-session override (two values that disagree, and the owner asked for one answer); persisting it in the database (it is a phone preference, not part of the collection, and it should not travel in a backup); remembering the filters too (not asked for, and a filter left on across launches hides species).

**D48** *(revised, see D50)* **— The edible tag covers game animals too, and nothing else about them changes.**
Why: the owner asked for game animals and insects to be marked as a food source so the filter finds them, which reverses D14's "the app stays out of it". The reasoning D14 gave — that whether an animal is edible is a hunting-and-fishing regulation question rather than a field-guide fact — is still true, and it is why the tag is *all* an animal gets: no curated note, because a season and a bag limit change every year and a stale one in an app is worse than none; no medicinal tag, because Duke's is a plant database and there is nothing behind such a claim; and the same disclaimer the plants carry, which already says this is documented use and not advice.
The rule the owner settled on, so a later curator can extend the list the same way: a licensed hunting or fishing season exists for the species in California, Oregon or Washington **and** it is commonly eaten — plus the insects with a real food tradition, which here are the two bees and the June beetle, taken as brood or grubs. That leaves out the marginal cases deliberately (raccoon, beaver, bat ray, leopard shark), and it leaves out the Monarch, which is poisonous. Twenty-three of the 120 animals carried it when the rule was written; after D50's expansion it is 49 of 224, applied to the new species by the same rule. The set is pinned by a test against the shipped asset, because nothing sourced decides it.
Rejected: tagging every insect as edible (it would mark the Monarch, whose milkweed cardenolides are exactly what makes it a bad thing to eat); a separate "Game" tag beside "Food source" (two filters for one question — the chip is already called Food source, and a fish is not game); renaming `PlantUse` to match (100 citations and two stored wire names for no user-visible gain — the enum keeps its name and says in its doc that it is no longer plants only).

**D49 — The one destructive button in Settings asks first.**
Why: the owner nearly cleared the picture caches by mis-tapping while scrolling Settings. Nothing is lost forever — every picture refetches — but the refetch is slow, silent and needs a connection, so the mistake reads exactly like the app breaking, across the whole dex at once. The dialog names the amount (because "clear the caches" means nothing until you know whether it is 2 MB or 200), names the cost as a refetch rather than a loss, and repeats what is *not* touched, which is the fear the button triggers. Its sentence is a pure function under test, like the export's.
It is the only confirmation in Settings on purpose. Export, import and the identification key are all additive or reversible in place; adding a step to those would teach the habit of dismissing dialogs, which is what makes the one that matters useless.
Rejected: an undo snackbar (there is nothing to undo — the bytes are gone the moment they are deleted, and "undo" would have to mean a re-download the user did not ask for); moving the button behind a long-press (undiscoverable, and it would still fire on a mis-press); a second confirmation on export (nothing destructive happens).

**D50 — The animal list grows to 224, chosen for what a walk actually turns up.**
Why: the owner asked to expand the animals to more of the common ones in the area, and settled the size at "maybe another 100". D1 picked 120 as the number that keeps a dex completable, and that reasoning still holds — but the gap it left was not at the aspirational end. It was at the ordinary one: the catalogue had the Bald Eagle and no Downy Woodpecker, the Orca and no Deer Mouse, the Dungeness Crab and no barnacle. A dex where the species you photograph on a normal afternoon is not in it teaches the user to stop looking.
So the 104 new species are picked by encounter rate rather than by charisma: 32 birds (dabbling ducks, grebes, egrets, gulls, the small woodpeckers, the everyday sparrows and finches), 14 mammals, 14 fish (the rest of the salmon, the rockfish and surfperch anyone fishing the coast lands), 6 reptiles, 5 amphibians, 18 insects and 15 other invertebrates — the mussel, the barnacle, the sea star, the pill bug. Every name was matched against the GBIF backbone before the build ran, which is what caught six that were drafted under superseded names.
Twenty-six of them meet D48's food-source rule, taking the tagged set from 23 to 49 — most of that is fish and shellfish, which is where the rule bites hardest and where the old list was thinnest.
The dex numbers stay per-kingdom and gapless, so the new animals are `#121`–`#224` and nothing already in the user's collection moves. The seven species the owner had already added by hand are deliberately *not* among them: a curated species with the same name would sit beside their own entry rather than replace it, and promoting a user-added species into the catalogue is C06, designed and not built.
Rejected: filling the gap with rarities to make the list look complete (a dex of things nobody sees is the failure mode D1 exists to prevent); renumbering so the new species interleave taxonomically (every stored `dexNumber` in the user's collection would move, and the unique index makes that an import that fails rather than one that quietly reorders); a second region instead (C03 is still the right shape for that, but it does not fix a thin list in the region the owner actually walks).

**D51 — The build's size checks come from the curated inputs, not from literals.**
Why: the expansion failed the build on its own success. `validate` asserted exactly 230 species, exactly 120 animals and animal numbers exactly 1..120 — so adding a species meant editing the guard, which teaches whoever hits it that the guard is an obstacle rather than a check. A guard nobody trusts catches nothing.
The counts now come from the four hand-authored input files, and the check becomes the one that was always meant: every species the curator listed reached the asset, and each kingdom numbers gaplessly from 1. That still fails on a dropped species, a duplicate number or a kingdom that lost its block — the failures the literals were there for — and it stops failing on a deliberate edit. The same change was made to the two tests that pinned the totals; the instrumented one now asserts the three kingdoms partition the asset rather than naming their sizes.
Rejected: bumping the literals with each expansion (the pattern that produced the failure); dropping the size check entirely (a silently truncated input file is exactly what it catches); reading the expected totals from `region.json` (a third place to keep in step, and the input files are already the source).

**D52 — An uncaught entry shows the dimmed picture, not the silhouette.**
Why: the owner opened an uncaught species from the grid and found a silhouette where the tile they had just tapped showed the animal. D41 dimmed the tile and deliberately left the entry withholding the picture — grid as reminder, entry as reward — and on the phone that split reads as a bug rather than as a rule. Tapping a picture should not take the picture away.
The reward the entry holds back is not the photograph. It is the caught date, the user's own captures, the tick and the full-colour picture; an uncaught hero drained to grey at 42% keeps every one of those. So the hero now runs the tile's rule: the same reference URL, the same zero-saturation filter, the same alpha — shared constants rather than two copies, because the unlock has to read as one picture gaining colour on both screens. The credit chip stays off a dimmed hero, since crediting a photograph the screen is not yet presenting reads as a reward already given.
An uncaught hero with nothing to draw falls back to the silhouette and says nothing about why. "Reference photo could not be loaded" under a species the user has not earned is a complaint about a picture they were not being shown, so every uncaught failure — no URL, offline, fetch failed — reports the same withheld state.
Rejected: dimming only the tile and leaving the entry alone (the split the owner reported); a lighter dim on the entry because the frame is bigger (two dim levels are two rules, and the grid-to-entry transition would flicker); showing the picture undimmed once the user has opened the entry (that *is* the reward, and giving it away for a tap empties the unlock).



**D37 — The edge is painted into the art, in the mask's own shape, sized against the 72dp the mask keeps.**
Why: the inset version shipped and looked wrong on the phone — a square photograph on a white tile, white wedges at the corners and a hairline along the sides. Two separate errors, and the second is the one worth remembering.

**A square inside a rounded mask cannot have an even edge.** The gap between a square and the squircle around it is widest at the diagonals and narrowest at the sides, so the "rim" reads as a tile the art was pasted onto. Only a shape that follows the mask gives a border of one width, which means the art has to carry the shape.

**And `inset` was measured against the wrong thing.** A bitmap foreground fills the 108dp layer and the mask keeps the central 72dp, so the art must be sized against 72dp — but `inset` resolves its 21dp against the layer bounds the launcher hands it, which are drawn smaller than 108dp of screen. The art landed near two thirds of the mask instead of the nine tenths the arithmetic promised.

D35 rejected painting the edge into the art because the launcher might not use the mask we assumed. That objection is real and this accepts it: the shape is a superellipse fitted to a measurement of the icon as this launcher actually renders it, so a launcher with a very different mask would cut the edge unevenly. What decided it is that the alternative does not work at all — leave a transparent margin in the foreground and the launcher scales the art back up until it fills the mask, so the edge disappears entirely. An even edge that assumes a squircle beats no edge on a single-user app that runs on one phone.

The measurement is the part to keep. The mask was read off by painting the art in bands of a different colour per tenth of the canvas, installing, and seeing which bands survived — after thin rings aliased at drawer size and were misread by a whole step, and after two builds were read as failures that were really the launcher's icon cache, which is keyed by package version and does not refresh on a same-version reinstall. `tools/icon/build_icon.py` holds the arithmetic and the procedure.
Rejected: shrinking the art to fit the edge inside the mask (the source is composed with bleed — the emblem sits in the middle and the wood around it is there to be cut — so scaling it down pulls the planks into view and the mark is lost in them); leaving the icon as it shipped (the owner asked for the mark with an outline, not a photo on a tile).

**D36 — Relatedness is a number of hops between two species in this catalogue, not a tree to browse.**
Why: three designs were mocked up and turned down before this one, and the rejections are the design. A browsable tree of life — indented, drill-down, and a sunburst — answered "where does this species sit", which nobody asked. A per-species cladogram answered the right question with more apparatus than the answer needed. What was actually wanted was "how far is a squirrel from a rabbit": a number.

The tree is `Life > kingdom > phylum > class > order > family > species`, so both ends of any comparison sit at depth 6 and the distance is twice the climb to their last shared rank. It is therefore always even and always one of six values, which is what makes it legible — a person learns the whole scale from six rows. Douglas Squirrel to Black-tailed Jackrabbit is 6.

**It measures over this catalogue, not over all of life.** Two species 6 apart here are not 6 apart in nature; the number answers "how far do I have to go, among the things I am collecting, before these two meet". That is the honest reading and it is the one the screen supports, because every species it names is one the user can go and find.

Two rules were found by building it rather than by reasoning about it, and both change the answer. **A rank both species lack is skipped** — GBIF's backbone gives no class to any ray-finned fish, and treating the gap as a mismatch put two salmon 8 apart while they sat in one family. A rank only one of them lacks does stop the walk, because carrying on would claim a shared ancestor we were never told about. **Equal distances break on the app's own class before dex number**: ties are the normal case rather than the exception — everything in one family is the same 2 hops away, and 119 animals are all exactly 10 from the Banana Slug — and dex order alone gave every distant species the same neighbours, whichever creatures held the low numbers. With the rule, a slug gets the sea star, the urchin and the crabs.

Three neighbours rather than five, because the list was never a ranking: which of thirteen equidistant pines surfaces is arbitrary, and a longer arbitrary list is only more of the same. A footnote carries the count that does mean something, which is how many share that distance.

The hop count sits inside a badge whose **shape** names the rank climbed to: circle, rounded square, square, diamond, hexagon, dotted ring. Three hexagons say "nothing close here" before a digit is read. Shape is never the only carrier — each row also spells the rank out and names the shared taxon — and the two nearest tiers take the accent while the rest recede.
Rejected: a browsable tree of the whole catalogue in any of its three forms (answers the wrong question, and the sunburst additionally puts most of 230 species in slivers a finger cannot hit); a lineage chain on the entry screen (cut by the owner — what is useful there is the way out to the comparison, not the path); genus as a rank in the count (201 genera behind 230 species, so it would separate almost nothing while pushing every number up by two); a fixed radius such as "everything in its family" (101 of the 230 species are the only member of theirs, so it shows nothing at all for nearly half the dex).

## 6. Screens

1. **Dex Grid** (home) — the Pacific USA catalogue as one grid in name order by default (D42), caught pictures in colour and uncaught ones dimmed, user-added species among them; search; a filter row with caught chips over Class / Ecosystem / Uses dropdowns (M23) and a Sort dropdown beside them (D32); a header with the BioDex title, the region pill, one progress pill per kingdom, and the Register and Settings buttons (D31).
2. **Entry Detail** — everything about one species: canonical picture (or the user's own where the entry prefers it — M46; or silhouette), the Stock photo / My photo toggle, ecosystems, the world range map (D34), habitat, then — for a plant with uses — the uses section with tags, note, caution and disclaimer, and nothing there for an animal or a plant without uses; then the user's photo strip, attribution, outbound link, Register button.
3. **Register a Species** — search pinned at the top, the catalogue (both kingdoms) scrolling beneath it, and the gallery photo row, Register button and "Add your own species" docked at the bottom; opens scrolled to a pre-selected species; optional Lens hand-off.
4. **Add Species — Confirm** — the confirmation card for a user-added species: the GBIF match with alternatives and its kingdom, found image and habitat text, the manual ecosystem multi-select; for a plant the growth-form pick, use toggles and uses note; accept, pick a different match, or edit by hand.
5. **Unlock Reveal** — the transient first-catch moment (overlay, not a destination); its counter names the kingdom.
6. **Photo Viewer** — one photo full-screen (resolved from its URI, or the unavailable state with re-link); date, place, note; delete and set-favorite live here.
7. **Nearest** (M43/D36) — one species at the top, then the three species in the catalogue closest to it: a shape-coded hop count, the name or a silhouette, and the rank and taxon they share. A footnote when more species sit at that same distance. Reached from the grid's top bar and from a link on an entry.
8. **Stats** — an overall meter per kingdom, per-ecosystem rows with an animal and a plant meter each, class bars grouped under Animals and Plants, recent catches.
9. **Settings** — export/import, "keep a local copy" toggle, cache management, licenses, About (with the foraging disclaimer).

Navigation: Dex Grid is home. Grid → Entry Detail (tap a cell). A Register button in the grid's top bar (see D31) and a Register action on uncaught detail screens → Register a Species → (Unlock Reveal →) Entry Detail of the registered species. Register → Add Species Confirm when the name isn't in the catalogue — and again later when a "details pending" entry backfills (M20). Detail → Photo Viewer (tap a photo). Stats and Settings are reachable from the grid's top bar, and so is Nearest, whose binary-tree glyph opens it on the most recent catch; an entry's own link opens it on that species. Back always returns to the previous screen; after registering, back from the detail screen returns to the grid.

## 7. Out of scope for v4

- Cloud sync, accounts, or any backend; any Google Photos API integration.
- Identification of animals and fungi — deferred until a provider is chosen; Google Lens remains the external tool. The app never tells the user what a mushroom is (D19).
- An on-device classifier of any kind: identification is a named third party's suggestion or it does not happen (D2).
- Foraging guidance beyond the documented-use tags, notes and the sourced medicinal line: no recipes, no dosages, no preparation instructions, no "is this safe" answers. Duke's phytochemical tables are not used.
- Video.
- Multi-user support, sharing, social features, leaderboards, trading.
- A second region (the model and the naming support it; the content doesn't ship).
- Further kingdoms beyond the three that ship — kelps and seaweeds (C07).
- In-app editing of the curated catalogue (user-added species are separate).
- A tablet or Wear OS layout; iOS.
- Gamification beyond the dex itself: XP, levels, streaks, daily quests.

## 8. Naming

- **BioDex** is the product. Written as one word with a capital B and capital D everywhere the user can read it: launcher label, app bar, share sheets, backup archive, About.
- A regional dex is **`<Region> BioDex`**: the shipped one is the **Pacific USA BioDex**. In the UI this is the "BioDex" title plus the "Pacific USA" region pill, never a single concatenated string, so a second region changes one pill.
- Kingdoms are **animals**, **plants** and **fungi** in the UI (meters, stats headings), lower-case in prose. They label no filter control of their own (D28).
- Plant classes are **trees, shrubs, herbs, ferns** as menu and bar labels; animal classes keep their existing labels.
- Uses are **Food source** and **Medicinal** as menu options and tags; the section heading is **Uses**.
- Numbers: animals `#001`, plants `P001`, user-added `U01`.
- Internally the package is `dev.tlong.biodex`, the database `biodex.db`, and the HTTP User-Agent `BioDex/1.0`; "Animal Dex" and `animaldex` do not survive anywhere in the codebase after the rename.

## 9. Open questions for the user

None that block the design. The two ecosystem renames (D16) and the plant list's composition — 40 trees / 18 shrubs / 19 herbs / 3 ferns as growth forms, with edible and medicinal as overlapping tags (ARCHITECTURE.md §11.3) — were both confirmed after the v4 draft. The one judgment worth a look once the pipeline report exists is the medicinal threshold of three distinct Duke's activities (D14): the report says how many of the 80 it tags, and the number can move without touching the design.
